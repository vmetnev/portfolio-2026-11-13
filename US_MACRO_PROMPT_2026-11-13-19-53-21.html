Introduction

Information cutoff: November 13, 2026 | Outlook horizon: through November 13, 2027

1. Executive findings

The evidence favors continued expansion, but with a more difficult inflation–policy trade-off—not a broad-based economic slowdown. Third-quarter domestic demand strengthened, particularly business investment. However, September household purchasing power weakened, and October inflation remained persistent. These conditions support revenues unevenly while limiting the case for easier Federal Reserve policy. This is an analytical interpretation, not a recession-probability estimate. (bea.gov)

The five most important changes in momentum are:

  1. Domestic demand accelerated: stronger investment accompanied resilient quarterly consumption.
  2. Consumer momentum weakened at the quarter’s exit: nominal spending growth increasingly reflected prices rather than additional consumption.
  3. Employment improved after earlier weakness: October hiring and upward revisions challenge a simple “continuing labor-market cooling” narrative.
  4. Inflation relief remained incomplete: the energy-price surge moderated in October, but core inflation persisted.
  5. The policy outlook shifted away from easing: official rate projections moved higher, while fiscal policy continued supporting selected demand categories but introduced a 2027 healthcare-policy headwind. (home.treasury.gov)

B. Consumer spending and income — reported estimates

Observation: September 2026. Published: October 29, 2026.

Nominal disposable income rose 0.2%, but real disposable income fell 0.3%. Nominal consumption increased 0.5%, while real consumption was approximately unchanged, following 0.5% real growth in August. The personal saving rate was 3.8%. (bea.gov)

Equity relevance/transmission: this is a weaker volume signal than the nominal spending figure suggests. Continued divergence between spending and real income would constrain discretionary purchases or require reduced saving and additional borrowing. Pricing-led revenue growth should therefore not automatically be interpreted as stronger unit demand.

Uncertainty: September is one month; it does not negate strong quarterly consumption. Aggregate income also conceals differences across households. (bea.gov)

Actual URL: https://bea.gov/news/2026/personal-income-and-outlays-september-2026

C. Household financial resilience — reported credit-panel data

Observation: Q3 2026. Published: November 10, 2026.

Household debt increased 1.8% to $19.1 trillion; credit-card balances reached approximately $1.29 trillion. Debt in some stage of delinquency increased to 5.0%, from 4.7%. Nevertheless, transitions into serious delinquency improved slightly for credit cards and student loans; mortgage deterioration remained low historically. (newyorkfed.org)

Equity relevance/transmission: household strain matters for discretionary retail volumes, consumer-finance charge-offs and collection costs. But these data do not establish a generalized household credit crisis: delinquency stocks worsened while several new-delinquency flows improved.

Uncertainty: nominal debt records alone are not measures of affordability; income, assets, loan terms and borrower distribution also matter. (newyorkfed.org)

Actual URL: https://www.newyorkfed.org/newsevents/news/research/2026/20261110

D. Investment, manufacturing and services — reported output and survey evidence

Observations: September industrial production; October purchasing-manager surveys. Publication dates: October 16, 2026 for industrial production; null for the undated ISM webpages retrieved.

  • Hard data: September industrial production rose 0.7%, but manufacturing increased only 0.3%; utilities rose 3.5%. The aggregate gain therefore overstated factory-specific acceleration. (federalreserve.gov)
  • Manufacturing survey: October ISM PMI increased to 55.5, from 54.5; production reached 59.0. Prices paid eased to 75.2, but remained elevated. (ismworld.org)
  • Services survey: October PMI eased to 54.5, from 54.9. New orders slowed to 55.9, from 59.8, while employment improved to 51.4. Prices paid remained high at 73.0. (ismworld.org)

Equity relevance/transmission: demand supports equipment, automation and infrastructure suppliers, but persistent input-cost pressure can erode margins where contracts lack repricing mechanisms. Services remain expansionary, although order momentum weakened.

Uncertainty: PMIs are diffusion surveys, not measured output-growth rates; slower deliveries can reflect supply constraints as well as strong demand.

E. Employment, unemployment and wages — reported estimates

Observation: October 2026, including revisions to August–September. Published: November 6, 2026.

Payrolls rose 165,000. August and September were revised upward by a combined 84,000, leaving September’s gain at 60,000. Unemployment was 4.1%, participation 61.9%, and average hourly earnings increased 3.1% year-over-year, although October’s monthly wage increase was 0.4%. (bls.gov)

Cooling remains visible beneath the headline: 27.6% of unemployed workers were long-term unemployed, and involuntary part-time employment increased 339,000. (bls.gov)

Equity relevance/transmission: job creation supports aggregate purchasing power; slower annual wage growth can relieve labor-intensive businesses’ costs. Conversely, difficult job finding and underemployment can weaken demand among marginal consumers.

Uncertainty: October is not proof of a sustained hiring acceleration. Federal Reserve Governor Bowman characterized conditions as low hiring and low firing, rather than a tight labor market. (federalreserve.gov)

Actual URLs: https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/empsit_11062026.htm https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/files/bowman20261110a.pdf

F. Headline and core inflation — reported data versus an official estimate

Observations: October CPI; September PCE prices. Published: November 10 and October 29, 2026, respectively.

  • October headline CPI rose 0.4% month-over-month, after 0.6% in September.
  • Core CPI increased 0.3%, its third consecutive increase of that size.
  • Shelter rose 0.3%, food 0.5%, energy 0.7%, and electricity 0.9%. Used vehicles fell 1.2%, providing an offset. (bls.gov)
  • September headline PCE inflation was 3.6% year-over-year and core PCE 3.1%. (bea.gov)

Important measurement gap: October headline and core CPI year-over-year changes were unavailable because October 2025 observations were missing following the appropriations lapse. No annual rate should be manufactured from that gap. (bls.gov)

Forecast, not realized data: on November 10, Bowman estimated October core PCE inflation at approximately 3.3% year-over-year. (federalreserve.gov)

Equity relevance/transmission: food, shelter and energy absorb household budgets; persistent core inflation also raises the hurdle for monetary easing. Revenue pricing benefits must be weighed against input inflation and volume elasticity.

Actual URLs: https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.htm https://bea.gov/news/2026/personal-income-and-outlays-september-2026 https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/files/bowman20261110a.pdf

G. Federal Reserve policy — decision and official assessments

Observation/decision date: October 28, 2026. Published: October 28, 2026.

The FOMC held its target range at 3.75%–4.00%, voting 10–2; both dissenters preferred a 25-basis-point increase. The statement described activity as expanding strongly, investment as robust and inflation as elevated. (federalreserve.gov)

Equity relevance/transmission: financing-sensitive demand and valuations cannot safely assume imminent easing. The policy risk is not simply weak growth; it is also resilient demand and persistent inflation requiring further restraint.

Uncertainty: Bowman attributed inflation pressures partly to tariffs, energy and AI-related demand. These are an official’s analytical assessments, not separately identified causal estimates established by the statistical releases. (federalreserve.gov)

Actual URL: https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20261028a.htm

High-valuation businesses dependent on distant earningsA higher official policy path raises valuation sensitivity even if near-term revenues remain sound. Strong macro growth alone therefore does not guarantee multiple expansion. (federalreserve.gov)

5. Contradictory evidence

  • Strong quarterly consumption versus weak September purchasing power: the quarter was resilient, but its exit momentum was weaker. Neither observation should replace the other. (home.treasury.gov)
  • Better hiring versus persistent labor-market friction: October payrolls and revisions improved, while long-term unemployment and involuntary part-time work remained concerns. (bls.gov)
  • Quarterly inflation moderation versus persistent monthly pressure: Q3 core PCE inflation slowed to 2.5% annualized, from 3.3%, but September PCE and October CPI showed less reassuring recent momentum. These measure different intervals. (bea.gov)
  • Worsening delinquency stocks versus improving flows: higher aggregate delinquency does not establish accelerating defaults across all loan categories. (newyorkfed.org)

6. Missing data and verification limitations

  • GDP revision risk: Q3 is an advance estimate. Bowman also warned that first-half GDP growth could be overstated by approximately 0.3 percentage point because revised retail data had not yet been incorporated. (federalreserve.gov)
  • Unavailable by cutoff: realized October PCE income/inflation and revised Q3 GDP were scheduled for November 25, 2026. October CPI annual comparisons were unavailable as explained above. (bea.gov)
  • Unquantified policy exposure: this research did not establish a comprehensive net 2027 fiscal impulse, current effective tariff schedule, or state-by-state Medicaid implementation effect. Those are evidence gaps—not findings that the effects are absent.
  • Source limitations: October deficit evidence is explicitly secondary; undated official/survey webpages have publication dates recorded as null. No post-cutoff releases or unverified market probabilities are used.
