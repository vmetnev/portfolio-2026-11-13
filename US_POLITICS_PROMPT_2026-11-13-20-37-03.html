1. Executive findings

  1. The midterms point to divided federal government beginning in January 2027—not an immediate change of administration. AP has called Democratic control of both congressional chambers, while Donald Trump remains president. Final congressional margins and state certifications remain incomplete in the evidence reviewed. The investment consequence is greater legislative bargaining and oversight, rather than automatic reversal of existing taxes, tariffs or regulations. (apnews.com)
  1. The most immediate fiscal catalyst is December 11, 2026, before the new Congress takes office. Enacted temporary funding expires that day. Defense increases, nondefense allocations and any additional reconciliation legislation must be distinguished from already appropriated funding. (govinfo.gov)
  1. Trade-policy risk persists despite the Supreme Court’s rejection of IEEPA tariffs. The administration subsequently implemented replacement Section 301 duties. European exporters therefore face a changed legal framework, not a general return to pre-2025 trading conditions. (supremecourt.gov)
  1. Data-center policy has become a material project-execution risk across party lines. Texas imposed an approval pause pending audits; New York established a one-year hyperscale moratorium; Pennsylvania removed AI data centers from expedited permitting. These are distinct measures with different scopes—not evidence of a nationwide construction ban. (gov.texas.gov)
  1. Implementation remains sector-specific. Bank stress-capital changes, Medicare reimbursement rules and power-plant deregulation have identifiable effective dates. Conversely, a large defense request, additional semiconductor export restrictions and campaign promises to restrict data centers are not equivalent to implemented policy. (federalreserve.gov)

1. Budget authority and borrowing

Fact: Public Law 119-103, enacted September 2, 2026, provides temporary FY2027 appropriations through December 11, 2026. Absent replacement legislation, the funding gap begins December 12. The outgoing Congress therefore retains a consequential funding decision before the January transition. (govinfo.gov)

Interpretation: Another continuing resolution would limit visibility for contractors and infrastructure suppliers; a shutdown could delay awards, permits and payments without necessarily cancelling underlying programs. A negotiated full-year package is the principal alternative. Monitor enacted appropriations and agency allotments—not negotiating announcements.

Reported fiscal observation: CBO’s November 9, 2026 report put the deficit for the fiscal year ended September 30, 2026 at $2.0 trillion, up $217 billion, or 12%, year over year and $140 billion above its February baseline estimate. This is a completed fiscal-year observation, not November’s borrowing run rate. (cbo.gov)

Transmission: financing requirements can affect discount rates and borrowing costs for leveraged businesses and long-duration equities. The direction of Treasury yields also depends on growth, inflation and monetary policy; the deficit alone does not establish causality.

2. Corporate taxation: existing investment deductions remain relevant

Fact: IRS guidance issued January 14, 2026 implements the 2025 law’s permanent 100% additional first-year depreciation for eligible property acquired after January 19, 2025. IRS guidance also confirms that, for tax years beginning after December 31, 2024, depreciation, amortization and depletion are added back when calculating adjusted taxable income for the business-interest limitation. These are enacted-law implementation measures, not new post-midterm promises. (content.govdelivery.com)

Interpretation: eligible capital-intensive businesses can receive earlier cash-tax deductions; the interest provision can improve deductibility for some borrowers. European companies may benefit through qualifying US operations. Benefits depend on taxable income, property eligibility and state conformity; they do not necessarily increase accounting earnings dollar-for-dollar. No new post-election corporate-rate change was established in this review.

Publication: January 14, 2026 for depreciation guidance; interest-page publication date null. Monitoring: final regulations, eligibility interpretations and state decoupling.

3. Tariffs: legal defeat followed by replacement measures

Court ruling: On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court held that IEEPA did not authorize the challenged presidential tariffs. (supremecourt.gov)

Implemented replacement: USTR announced Section 301 duties on July 23, effective July 24, 2026, of 10% or 12.5%, subject to product exemptions and country-specific treatment. For specified EU and other partner products, the applicable calculation is net of the most-favored-nation rate; these percentages should not simply be added to every product’s existing duty. (ustr.gov)

Interpretation: import-dependent US retailers and manufacturers face landed-cost pressure; European exporters face pricing, volume and localization decisions. Protected US production can gain relative competitiveness, but imported inputs can offset that advantage. Monitor customs instructions, product classifications, exemptions and litigation. The alternative outcome is selective relief—not necessarily wholesale tariff removal.

5. Contradictory evidence and alternative outcomes

  • “Opposition victory means immediate policy reversal” is unsupported. Existing tax deductions, executive trade authorities and finalized rules remain separate legal instruments; congressional control primarily changes bargaining and oversight. (irs.gov)
  • “One party is uniformly pro- or anti-data-center” is contradicted by implemented actions. Republican-led Texas and Democratic-led New York and Pennsylvania all imposed material constraints, with different scopes. (gov.texas.gov)
  • “Restrictions imply an investment collapse” is not established. Semiconductor awards and disbursements continue, while energy-permitting legislation advanced. Projects may be delayed, redesigned or relocated rather than cancelled. (gao.gov)
  • “Deregulation uniformly raises earnings” is too strong. Bank-buffer smoothing is not necessarily a lower requirement for every firm; generator relief does not eliminate grid bottlenecks; utility large-load protections coexist with rejected rate increases. (govinfo.gov)
