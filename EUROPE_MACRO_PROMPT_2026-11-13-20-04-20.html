European economic environment: evidence report

Introduction

Information cutoff: 13 November 2026 | Outlook horizon: to 13 November 2027

Classification: Reported = published data or policy decisions; Forecast = an institution’s projection; Interpretation = analytical inference. Annual forecasts for 2027 are not equivalent to forecasts for the exact rolling 12-month horizon.

1. Executive findings

  • The euro area is expanding, but the recovery is uneven and losing sequential momentum. Third-quarter growth slowed from the second quarter; Spain substantially outperformed Germany, while Italy stagnated and France contracted. Aggregate European growth therefore provides a poor guide to individual companies’ domestic revenue prospects. (ec.europa.eu)
  • Renewed inflation is predominantly an energy shock—not yet a generalized wage-price acceleration. October headline inflation reached 4.0%, against 2.5% excluding energy, food, alcohol and tobacco. Nevertheless, higher energy costs threaten household purchasing power and corporate margins, while limiting monetary-policy relief. (ec.europa.eu)
  • The investment outlook is divided: defense, infrastructure and digital investment offer support, but tighter credit and weaker loan demand constrain private capital expenditure. Germany’s fiscal support contrasts with assumed French consolidation and the approaching withdrawal of Next Generation EU support, particularly relevant to Italy. (ecb.europa.eu)
  • The US has stronger demand momentum and a gas-cost advantage. European exporters can benefit from US demand, but European production locations remain exposed to an unfavorable energy-cost differential. The UK is a separate case: stronger recent growth, but higher policy rates and a worsening official inflation forecast. (bea.gov)

B. Manufacturing, services and business investment

2. Manufacturing remains weaker than services — Reported. Published: 15 October and 9 November 2026, respectively. Observation: August 2026.

Euro-area industrial production fell 0.5% month-on-month. Services production rose 0.3% month-on-month and 2.2% year-on-year. Within services, information and communication output increased 5.9% annually, whereas accommodation and food services declined 0.1%. Transmission: a general “services resilience” thesis should not be extended automatically to hospitality, nor industrial weakness to digital services. (ec.europa.eu)

3. Credit conditions are becoming less supportive of capital expenditure — Reported survey. Published: 27 October 2026. Observation: Q3 2026; survey conducted 21 September–6 October.

A net 9% of banks tightened corporate credit standards, versus 7% in Q2. Corporate loan demand recorded a net decline of 6%, following a 3% increase. Banks in France and Germany tightened standards; Italy eased and Spain was unchanged. Transmission: financing-sensitive investment and working capital face headwinds, but the intensity differs by country. Survey balances measure banks’ responses, not percentage changes in lending volumes. (ecb.europa.eu)

Source URL: https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/pr/date/2026/html/ecb.pr261027~506ef03145.pt.html

4. Private-investment expectations have weakened — Forecast, business-association evidence. Published: 12 November 2026. Forecast period: calendar 2026.

BusinessEurope reduced its euro-area investment-growth forecast to 1.7%, from 2.4% in spring, citing energy uncertainty and tightening financing conditions. This is the association’s forecast—not an official national-accounts observation or market consensus. Transmission: positive investment growth remains plausible, but the downward revision weakens the case for a broad capital-goods earnings acceleration. (businesseurope.eu)

Source URL: https://www.businesseurope.eu/publications/businesseurope-economic-outlook-autumn-2026-short-term-resilience-masks-inflationary-and-fiscal-risks/

C. Consumers, employment and wages

5. Consumer goods demand is fragile — Reported, first estimates. Published: 6 November 2026. Observation: September 2026.

Euro-area retail volumes fell 0.7% month-on-month, following a revised 0.4% rise in August, and were only 0.4% higher annually. Non-food volumes fell 1.2% monthly. Germany’s retail volumes declined 2.4% annually; Spain’s declined 1.5%, despite its stronger GDP growth. Transmission: nominal sales growth may conceal weak volumes, increasing promotional pressure and limiting fixed-cost absorption. Retail data do not cover all household services spending. (ec.europa.eu)

Source URL: https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/products-euro-indicators/w/4-06112026-ap

6. Labor-market support is weakening, not collapsing — Reported. Published: 30 October 2026. Observation: September 2026.

Euro-area unemployment was 6.5%, unchanged from revised August data but above 6.3% a year earlier. The number unemployed increased by 122,000 monthly. Transmission: slowing job creation and rising unemployment reduce the scope for consumption to overcome the energy-related real-income squeeze; they may also moderate recruitment costs. (ec.europa.eu)

Source URL: https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/products-euro-indicators/w/3-30102026-bp

7. Wage pressure has moderated — Reported and forward-looking indicator. Published: 29 October 2026. Observations: compensation in Q2 2026; wage tracker through Q3 2027.

The ECB reported compensation-per-employee growth of 3.3% in Q2, down from 3.6% in Q1; improving productivity contained unit-labor-cost growth. Its wage tracker indicated negotiated-pay growth averaging 2.6% over the first three quarters of 2027. Transmission: moderating labor costs can protect margins, but subdued pay growth alongside elevated energy inflation constrains household purchasing power. The tracker is not a guarantee of realized economy-wide wages. (ecb.europa.eu)

Source URL: https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/press_conference/monetary-policy-statement/2026/html/ecb.is261029~5c5ffe99e5.en.html

9. The ECB is not offering a predetermined easing path — Reported policy and official guidance. Published/decision: 29 October 2026. Policy observation: applicable at the cutoff.

The ECB held its deposit rate at 2.50%, following September’s 25-basis-point increase. APP and PEPP holdings continue to decline without reinvestment. Guidance remains data-dependent and meeting-by-meeting, with no committed rate path. Transmission: investors should not assume that weak growth automatically produces cheaper financing; persistent energy inflation can keep borrowing costs and valuation discount rates elevated. (ecb.europa.eu)

10. Official recovery forecasts are conditional on energy normalization — Forecast. Published: 10 September 2026. Forecast periods: 2026–2028; technical assumptions cut off 19 August.

The ECB projected 1.4% GDP growth in 2027, headline inflation of 2.5%, and inflation excluding energy and food of 2.6%. However, its 29 October statement said energy prices had subsequently exceeded the September baseline. Relevance: the September forecast is an official reference case, not an updated assessment incorporating all information available on 13 November. (ecb.europa.eu)

E. Fiscal support, infrastructure and defense

11. Fiscal support is geographically and temporally uneven — Official assessment and forecasts.

  • Germany: published 8 October 2026, covering 2026–2028. The government identified exports and state defense/infrastructure investment as growth drivers, but forecast growth of only 1.1% in 2027, versus 1.3% in 2026. This supports public-order demand without establishing a self-sustaining private-investment boom. (bundesregierung.de)
  • Euro-area composition: the ECB’s 10 September 2026 forecast expects AI, defense and infrastructure spending to support investment, while NGEU expiry slows public-investment growth in 2027–2028. Its forecast also assumes further French budget savings and reduced NGEU support in Italy. These are forecast assumptions, not proof that every proposed fiscal measure will be enacted or executed. (ecb.europa.eu)
  • Ukraine-related expenditure: at its 15–16 October 2026 meeting, the European Council welcomed ongoing disbursements under the €90 billion support loan for 2026–2027. This establishes financing activity, but the full amount should not be treated as incremental orders or revenues for European defense manufacturers. (consilium.europa.eu)

F. Energy costs and international competitiveness

12. Winter gas security remains a material downside risk — Reported conditions and conditional scenarios. Published: 3 November 2026. Observations: principally Q1–Q3 2026 and end-October storage; outlook winter 2026–2027.

The IEA identified disrupted LNG flows through the Strait of Hormuz and unusually weak European storage buffers. End-October storage fill was 62% in Germany and 66% in the Netherlands, leaving northwestern Europe particularly exposed. Its benchmark comparison also shows US Henry Hub gas substantially cheaper than European TTF. Transmission: European energy-intensive production faces cost and working-capital disadvantages; winter weather and supply restoration could materially change outcomes. Benchmark gas prices are not equivalent to companies’ hedged, delivered energy costs. (iea.org)

Source URL: https://www.iea.org/files/42bb838f-d20d-4815-8554-32146033967d/GlobalGasSecurityReview2026.pdf

G. UK and US comparisons

13. UK growth is stronger, but the policy/inflation mix is less favorable — Reported and forecast.

  • ONS publication: 12 November 2026. Observation: Q3 2026. GDP increased 0.7% quarter-on-quarter, following 0.5%, principally reflecting household consumption and gross fixed capital formation. (ons.gov.uk)
  • BoE publication: 5 November 2026. Decision: meeting ending 4 November. The Bank raised Bank Rate to 4.0%, with eight members supporting 25 basis points and one preferring 50. September CPI inflation was 3.5%; the Bank forecast approximately 5% by mid-2027, while noting limited evidence so far of energy spillovers into other prices and wages. Transmission: stronger demand supports revenues, but higher financing and energy costs threaten subsequent household spending and margins. (bankofengland.co.uk)

14. US demand momentum exceeds the euro area’s — Reported. Published: 29 October 2026. Observation: Q3 2026, advance estimate.

US GDP grew 0.8% quarter-on-quarter, or 3.4% annualized, supported by investment, consumption and government spending. The comparable euro-area quarterly rate was 0.4%—not 0.4% versus 3.4%. The Fed’s 28 October decision maintained its target range at 3.75%–4.00%, above the ECB deposit rate; nominal rates alone do not establish relative policy restrictiveness. Transmission: US-facing European businesses have a stronger external-demand backdrop than many purely domestic businesses, but financing and production-location differences remain material. (bea.gov)

3. Changes in direction or momentum

  • Activity: euro-area growth slowed in Q3, while Germany decelerated, France contracted and Italy stalled. Spain remained stronger; UK growth accelerated. This is divergence, not synchronized European contraction. (ec.europa.eu)
  • Households: retail volumes weakened and employment stopped growing quarterly; unemployment was above its year-earlier rate. The income foundation for consumption is becoming less supportive. (ec.europa.eu)
  • Prices and policy: headline inflation accelerated while core inflation remained stable. The relevant policy question has shifted toward the persistence and pass-through of the energy shock—not simply the timing of rate cuts. (ec.europa.eu)
  • Investment: credit tightening and reduced investment forecasts contrast with targeted public and digital spending support. (ecb.europa.eu)

5. Contradictory evidence

  • Weak industry data do not prove an imminent recession. The ECB’s November bulletin reported October manufacturing and services PMI readings above 50, although both had weakened relative to Q3. These private surveys, reproduced by the ECB, conflict with a uniformly contractionary interpretation of the hard data. (ecb.europa.eu)
  • Strong national GDP does not guarantee strong consumer-facing earnings: Spain combined relatively rapid GDP growth with falling annual retail volumes. (ec.europa.eu)
  • High headline inflation does not yet establish a wage-price spiral: euro-area core inflation was stable and wage growth moderated; the BoE likewise reported limited second-round evidence. (ec.europa.eu)
  • Fiscal support is not equivalent to immediate domestic earnings: timing, import content and expiring EU funding can dilute or offset announced spending. (ecb.europa.eu)

6. Missing data and verification limitations

  • Q3 GDP/employment and October inflation remain preliminary. Detailed Q3 expenditure contributions were not established here; aggregate growth cannot safely be attributed entirely to consumption or investment. (ec.europa.eu)
  • August industrial data are lagged observations, not November conditions. Eurostat’s next industrial-production release was scheduled for 16 November 2026, after the cutoff. (ec.europa.eu)
  • A complete, independently verified comparison of enacted 2027 national budgets, actual infrastructure/defense disbursements, UK underlying inflation/pay, current tariff treatment and exchange-rate effects was not obtained. These are evidence gaps, not findings that the effects are absent.
  • Company-level energy hedges, procurement exposure, geographic sales and refinancing schedules were not collected; consequently, no numerical earnings effects or valuation targets are inferred.
  • All cited substantive evidence is primary institutional evidence, except BusinessEurope’s explicitly identified association forecast and the private PMI surveys reproduced by the ECB.
