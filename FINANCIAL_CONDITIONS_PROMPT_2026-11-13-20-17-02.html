Financial conditions affecting US and European equities

Introduction

Information cutoff: November 13, 2026. Observations below use their actual dates—not an assumed November 13 market close. European coverage is principally the euro area.

1. Executive findings

  • US conditions are mixed, rather than uniformly tightening. Real yields increased in early November, and banks tightened credit for smaller businesses, commercial real estate and nonbank financial institutions. Conversely, corporate credit spreads narrowed in the latest available week, while Federal Reserve reserve-management policy supports banking-system liquidity. Interpretation: financing remains more difficult for weaker borrowers than for large, established issuers. (fred.stlouisfed.org)
  • Euro-area financing shows broader tightening through lending availability and sovereign-risk transmission. Banks reported additional tightening, weaker corporate loan demand and deteriorating funding access. Recent corporate-spread compression provides some relief, but does not reverse those lending constraints. (ecb.europa.eu)
  • The retrieved evidence does not establish a generalized funding crisis. US financial-stress indicators deteriorated but remained below their historical-average benchmarks. Distress is observable among weaker corporate borrowers; verified, comparable current default-rate data remain a material gap. (fred.stlouisfed.org)

Important distinction: policy rates set overnight monetary conditions; government yields price market borrowing across maturities; credit spreads add borrower-risk compensation; lending standards determine whether financing is available at all. These measures need not move together. (federalreserve.gov)

2. Dated observations and source references

A. Policy rates—not equivalent to corporate borrowing costs

Reported facts

  • Federal Reserve: held the federal-funds target at 3.75–4.00% on October 28; two dissenters preferred a 25-basis-point increase.
  • ECB: deposit rate 2.50%, following increases effective June 17 and September 16; the October 29 decision left rates unchanged. (federalreserve.gov)

Equity transmission—interpretation: floating-rate borrowers face relatively direct policy-rate exposure; fixed-rate issuers primarily face repricing when refinancing. An unchanged policy rate does not establish unchanged financing conditions. (federalreserve.gov)

B. Government yields and curves

US reported facts: Treasury’s November 12 snapshot showed 3-month 4.32%, 2-year 4.86%, 10-year 5.10%, and 30-year 5.43%. Calculated from those observations, 10-year minus 2-year was +24 bp, and 10-year minus 3-month +78 bp: an upward-sloping curve, not an inversion. The complete historical Treasury table was inaccessible, limiting verification of recent curve changes. (home.treasury.gov)

European reported facts: during September 10–October 28, the GDP-weighted euro-area 10-year sovereign yield rose 8 bp to approximately 4.0%, although 10-year OIS fell from 3.4% to 3.2%. France’s sovereign/OIS spread widened 59 bp. Thus, lower swap rates did not translate into lower sovereign financing costs. (ecb.europa.eu)

A November 13 Stuttgart exchange snapshot also showed an upward-sloping German government curve: 2-year 3.122%, 5-year 3.165%, 9-year 3.323%. These quoted maturity points should not be treated as identical to Treasury constant-maturity par yields. (boerse-stuttgart.de)

Equity transmission—interpretation: higher market yields raise financing hurdles and discount rates; sovereign-spread widening adds pressure on locally exposed banks and borrowers even when OIS declines. (ecb.europa.eu)

C. Real yields

Reported fact: the latest verified primary-source US 10-year inflation-indexed yield was 2.78% on November 10, versus 2.66% November 9 and 2.63% November 6—a 15 bp increase from November 6. This is not a November 13 observation. (fred.stlouisfed.org)

Equity transmission—interpretation: the increase raises the real financing/discount-rate hurdle, particularly for businesses whose expected cash flows lie far in the future. Comparable recent European real-yield observations were not verified; no nominal-yield-minus-current-inflation substitute is used. (fred.stlouisfed.org)

E. Bank lending standards and availability

US—reported survey results: Q3 standards were broadly unchanged for large/middle-market C&I borrowers but tightened for small firms. Banks tightened standards across CRE categories and queried nonbank-financial-institution categories. Most ordinary C&I terms eased or were unchanged, illustrating the difference between loan pricing and approval availability. Corporate loan demand weakened. (federalreserve.gov)

Euro area—reported survey results: net corporate tightening rose to 9% of banks from 7% in Q2, exceeding the previously expected 5%. Corporate loan demand shifted from +3% to −6%. SME standards tightened more than large-company standards, and bank funding access deteriorated. Forecast, not fact: banks expected another net tightening in Q4. (ecb.europa.eu)

Equity transmission—interpretation: restricted approvals, collateral requirements and credit-line availability constrain working capital and investment even where quoted spreads improve. Smaller and bank-dependent companies are most directly exposed. (federalreserve.gov)

Publication / observation: SLOOS: November 2 / broadly Q3 2026, surveyed September 21–October 5. ECB BLS: publication null / Q3, surveyed September 21–October 6. URLs: https://www.federalreserve.gov/data/documents/sloos-202610.pdf https://www.ecb.europa.eu/stats/ecb_surveys/bank_lending_survey/html/ecb.blssurvey2026q3~c6a731e213.da.html

F. Refinancing and default-related evidence

Reported observations

  • US banks reported stronger nonbank demand associated partly with liquidity and refinancing needs, while tightening nonbank lending standards. This suggests refinancing demand is not synonymous with ready credit availability. (federalreserve.gov)
  • Secondary evidence: Northern Trust’s October 30 commentary reported $73 billion of US HY bonds trading above 1,000-bp spreads in September, 5.2% of the universe versus 4.5% a year earlier. Seven issuers represented 41% of that distressed balance. Distressed pricing is a warning signal—not a realized default rate. (advisorperspectives.com)
  • A November 9 ECB supervisory overview stated that corporate insolvencies were rising. Risk assessment, not an observed outcome: it warned that bank asset quality could deteriorate. (bankingsupervision.europa.eu)

Equity transmission—interpretation: near-term maturities, weak interest coverage and reliance on refinancing increase the risk of higher interest expense, investment cuts, dilution or restructuring. Current matched maturity/coupon data were insufficient to quantify aggregate refinancing cost increases. (advisorperspectives.com)

Publication / observation: Northern Trust: October 30 / September 2026. ECB overview: November 9 / observation period null. G. Central-bank balance sheets and liquidity

Federal Reserve—reported facts

  • November 11 total assets: $6.759 trillion, up $10.7 billion week over week.
  • Weekly-average reserves: $2.961 trillion, up $30.5 billion; weekly-average Treasury General Account: down $42.8 billion.
  • Central-bank liquidity swaps outstanding: approximately $171 million. These aggregates alone cannot establish liquidity distribution across institutions. (federalreserve.gov)

The October 28 implementation directive authorizes Treasury-bill purchases when appropriate to maintain ample reserves, rolls over Treasury principal, and reinvests agency principal into bills. Interpretation: this is reserve-management support; it should not automatically be labelled a new macroeconomic QE programme. (federalreserve.gov)

Euro area—reported facts: excess liquidity fell approximately €22 billion to €2.095 trillion over September 10–October 28; APP/PEPP portfolios continued declining without principal reinvestment. Banks separately reported mildly negative portfolio-runoff effects on liquidity and market financing. (ecb.europa.eu)

Equity transmission—interpretation: reserve support can cushion money-market funding, while runoff can increase private-sector balance-sheet absorption. Neither establishes that corporate lending is plentiful. (federalreserve.gov)

Publication / observation: H.4.1: November 12 / November 11 point-in-time and week-ended averages. Implementation note: October 28 / effective directive. ECB dates as above. H. USD and EUR

Reported facts: between November 2 and November 6, the Fed’s broad nominal dollar index rose from 121.3702 to 121.8803; EUR/USD declined from $1.1204 to $1.1154 per euro. These are the latest verified observations from these weekly-published series, not November 13 spot quotes. (fred.stlouisfed.org)

Equity transmission—interpretation: dollar strengthening increases the euro cost of unhedged USD debt and dollar-priced inputs. It can support euro exporters’ translated revenues while reducing US companies’ translated foreign earnings. Revenue matching and hedges can substantially alter these effects; spot FX alone does not measure dollar-funding scarcity. (fred.stlouisfed.org)

Publication / observation: November 9 / November 2–6, 2026. URLs: https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DTWEXBGS https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DEXUSEU

3. Changes in direction or momentum

  • Latest week: credit-spread easing contrasts with rising US real yields—opposing movements in two components of financing conditions. (fred.stlouisfed.org)
  • Quarterly lending: euro-area tightening broadened; US tightening remained more borrower-specific. Survey frequency means these results cannot establish daily changes after the survey windows. (ecb.europa.eu)
  • Liquidity: US reserve-management support contrasts with continuing euro-area portfolio runoff. That divergence is not evidence that either region’s private credit supply is uniformly easing or contracting. (federalreserve.gov)

4. Implications for businesses and equity markets

Analytical interpretations—not earnings forecasts:

Company characteristicsPrincipal transmission
Highly leveraged, floating-rate or near-maturity debtFaster interest-expense repricing; refinancing and restructuring risk.
SMEs, CRE developers and bank-dependent borrowersApproval, collateral and credit-line constraints can reduce investment and working capital.
Long-duration growth businesses and capital-intensive projectsHigher real discount rates and financing hurdles pressure valuations and project economics.
Banks and nonbank lendersHigher asset yields may help income, but funding costs, borrower weakness and sovereign exposures can offset that benefit.
Unhedged USD borrowers with EUR revenuesCurrency mismatch raises debt-service costs; natural hedges and derivatives matter.

These sensitivities follow from the observed real-yield, lending, distress, sovereign-risk and FX evidence; they are not security selections or portfolio recommendations. (fred.stlouisfed.org)

5. Contradictory evidence

  • Against a generalized credit crunch: recent US IG/HY and euro HY spread compression; broadly unchanged US large-company lending standards. (fred.stlouisfed.org)
  • Against uniformly easy conditions: tighter small-business/CRE lending, rising European insolvencies and deteriorating euro-area bank funding access. (federalreserve.gov)
  • Against a systemic-stress conclusion: below-average US stress-index levels and concentrated HY distress. Neither rules out severe issuer-specific problems. (fred.stlouisfed.org)

6. Missing data and verification limitations

  • Defaults: no sufficiently verified, contemporaneous, comparable US/euro-area HY-bond and leveraged-loan default series was retrieved. Insolvencies and distressed prices must not be substituted for default rates.
  • Refinancing: insufficient matched maturity schedules, existing coupons, hedges and new-issue concessions to quantify aggregate earnings effects.
  • European real yields and geography: reliable recent real-yield curves were not verified; UK, Swiss and Nordic funding coverage is incomplete.
  • Funding stress: recent cross-currency basis, repo-rate dispersion, facility usage and European collateral-market data were not comprehensively verified. Dollar scarcity cannot be inferred from FX appreciation.
  • Timing/access: Treasury’s full historical table was inaccessible; some sources expose only snapshots. Missing evidence is not evidence of no stress.
  • No equity risk premium was calculated, and equity-market performance was not used as a liquidity proxy.
